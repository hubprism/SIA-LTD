Climate & Resilience

Resilience and adaptation: why they're not the same thing

Governments talk about "climate resilience" as if it were interchangeable with "adaptation." It isn't — and the confusion between them is one of the most common (and costly) mistakes institutions make when developing a climate strategy.

By The SIA Team · August 2026

Adaptation is a specific action: adjusting a system, a piece of infrastructure, or a livelihood in response to a known or expected climate impact, such as a sea wall, a drought-resistant crop, or a redesigned drainage system. Each is discrete and targeted.

Resilience is broader: the capacity of a system to absorb a shock, continue functioning, and recover without collapsing. It's the cumulative result of many adaptation measures working together, supported by institutional coordination, trust, and functioning communication networks.

A real case: Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme
Bangladesh's cyclone history shows clear progress. The 1970 Bhola cyclone killed 300,000–500,000 people, and the 1991 cyclone caused 138,000-147,000 deaths. By contrast, Cyclone Remal in May 2024 resulted in only 16-18 deaths, despite affecting 4.6 million people. Over 800,000 people evacuated to shelters. This decline highlights Bangladesh's investments in early warning, community readiness, and large-scale evacuations, which have significantly improved protection.

This outcome does not stem from a single adaptation strategy. It is the result of Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme (CPP) — a collaborative initiative involving the government, the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society, and global partners, centred on thousands of trained local volunteers, an early-warning communication network, and shelters that have been tested through consistent use. Research into the programme's more recent performance also found that pairing early warning with community-based health preparedness — clean water provision and mobile medical units embedded directly in the evacuation process — reduced post-cyclone diarrhoeal disease outbreaks by roughly 40% compared with the 2007 Cyclone Sidr response. That's resilience: adaptation measures functioning as a coordinated system rather than a list of independent projects.

It is important to highlight the same research's findings on equity: warning messages reached male-headed and farming households at rates 11–13 percentage points higher than those for female-headed and fishing households. Even a resilience system as effective as Bangladesh's still has to keep closing gaps in who the system actually protects — a reminder that resilience isn't just a technical achievement; it's an ongoing equity commitment.

Why "adaptation" alone isn't a resilience strategy
In practice, most government climate programmes are a portfolio of individual adaptation projects — a flood defence here, a drought-resilient seed programme there — commissioned and evaluated one at a time. Bangladesh's experience shows what happens when those pieces are instead designed to function as a single system: warning, shelter, evacuation and health response reinforce each other rather than operating in isolation.

Climate finance readiness increasingly reflects this shift. Resilience facilities and adaptation funds are moving towards rewarding applicants who demonstrate systemic thinking, rather than a disconnected list of adaptation projects competing for the same budget line.

Training & Monitoring & Evaluation

Beyond Training: Building Capability Through MEL

Most institutions often claim their training programmes are effective based on attendance sheets and satisfaction surveys. However, this doesn't prove impact; it merely shows that participants attended.

By The SIA Team · September 2026

A real case: West Africa's regional research training programme
Between 2008 and 2013, the West African Health Organisation (WAHO) held various workshops in ECOWAS nations such as Burkina Faso, The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau, Togo, and Liberia. These sessions focused on research methodology and trained 84 health researchers and programme managers. A peer-reviewed evaluation in BMC Medical Education (2021) offers a candid assessment of the training's outcomes and limitations.

On metrics closest to the training room, the programme was clearly successful: participant knowledge scores rose from under 50% correct on the pre-test to well over 50% on the post-test at nearly every session, and 64.3% of trainees went on to actively conduct research afterwards — well above what typical one-off workshops achieve. Sixteen research protocols were funded, and fourteen were fully implemented.

But the evaluation also tracked what happened next — and that's where the gap opened. Of those fourteen completed projects, only two were published as scientific articles, only two were shared through national dissemination workshops, and only two demonstrably influenced a real programme decision (a national sickle-cell disease programme in Niger and emergency-service reorganisation at a hospital in Côte d'Ivoire). The evaluators' own conclusion was direct: training built knowledge and even research practice, but the programme hadn't built the modules — in data analysis, scientific writing, and knowledge transfer — needed to get that evidence into use. Capacity was built. The pipeline from capacity to institutional decision-making wasn't.

What this shows about training design
Effective MEL shouldn't be an afterthought following training — it must be integrated from the beginning. It should assess not only if individuals learned, but also if the organisation created a pathway for that knowledge to inform decision-making.

  • Baseline assessment — what can participants do before training starts?
  • Practice-linked design — real protocols, real data, not hypothetical exercises.
  • Dissemination built into scope — publication, internal reporting, and knowledge-transfer support budgeted from day one, not treated as optional extras.
  • Follow-up tracking — whether findings actually changed a programme decision, not merely whether a report was filed.

The WAHO evaluation makes the case better than any hypothetical could: even a well-designed, well-evaluated training programme can build real skills yet still under-deliver on impact if the MEL framework around it stops at "was the training delivered?" rather than "did the evidence produced ever reach a decision-maker?"

Where SIA's niche sits
This is why we treat training and MEL as an integrated capability rather than two separate service lines. A training programme we design includes an evaluation and dissemination framework built in from day one — not just pre- and post-knowledge testing, but a defined pathway to ensure findings reach the people who can act on them.

Environmental Health

Environmental health risks, climate change, and the populations most vulnerable to exposure

Climate change is usually discussed in terms of temperature and emissions. But its most immediate consequences are health-related — and they don't fall evenly. The populations already carrying the greatest burden of health inequality are, almost without exception, the same populations facing the sharpest environmental health risk.

By The SIA Team · July 2026

A real case: Ogoniland, Niger Delta
In 2011, the United Nations Environment Programme published its Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland, a comprehensive fourteen-month investigation commissioned by the Nigerian government. The assessment covered more than 200 sites, analysed over 4,000 samples, reviewed more than 5,000 medical records, and engaged consultations with over 23,000 individuals from the affected communities.

The findings were unequivocal. In the community of Ogale, groundwater was found to be contaminated with benzene, a known human carcinogen, at levels hundreds of times above World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines. These concentration levels were sufficiently severe for UNEP to recommend the urgent provision of alternative drinking water prior to any other remediation measures. The report further disclosed that years of inadequate maintenance of oilfield infrastructure have resulted in contamination spreading more extensively and deeper than previously recognised, thereby posing a substantial risk to public health from contaminated drinking water and carcinogenic substances in several regions. UNEP estimated that complete ecological restoration of the area could require 25 to 30 years.

This exemplifies environmental health inequality in its starkest form: a community situated directly above the resource that generates considerable national wealth, yet bearing some of the highest documented levels of health exposure in the country, with remediation efforts still incomplete more than a decade after the assessment was published. The Nigerian government established the Hydrocarbon Pollution Remediation Project (HYPREP) in response; however, its implementation has faced persistent delays — a pattern that underscores a second, equally important lesson: recognising health inequality does not equate to eradicating it. Sustained institutional commitment is as crucial as the initial assessment.

Why this matters beyond one region
The pattern isn't unique to the Niger Delta. Communities closest to industrial or extractive activity are consistently those with the least healthcare infrastructure, the least political leverage to demand remediation, and the least financial capacity to relocate or absorb health costs. Climate change compounds this further — rising temperatures, floods, shifting disease patterns, and water stress hit populations already carrying the greatest pre-existing health burden hardest. The inequality isn't created by any single event. It's revealed and worsened by it.

What this means for impact assessment and compliance
For institutions and corporations undertaking high-impact projects, the Ogoniland case has direct practical implications.

  • Baseline health assessments need a climate and equity lens — not just current exposure levels, but also pre-existing disparities in healthcare access and infrastructure quality within the affected population.
  • Assessment without a credible remediation and monitoring pathway is incomplete — the Ogoniland case shows that even a rigorous, internationally credible assessment can stall for years without sustained institutional accountability.
  • Stakeholder consultation must reach the populations most exposed, not just the most accessible or the most vocal.
  • Compliance frameworks are tightening, with regulators and financiers increasingly expecting climate-adjusted, equity-aware health risk analysis to be standard, not exceptional, practice.

Where SIA's niche sits
This is precisely why environmental health, climate risk and social equity aren't run as separate service lines for us — and why we don't treat an assessment as the finish line. A credible environmental health risk assessment must be read in light of the pre-existing inequalities that shape who bears the risk, and it must include a realistic, monitored pathway to remediation, not just a report.

For any organisation undertaking a project with real exposure — industrial, infrastructure, extractive, or otherwise — the standard has shifted. Health, climate, and equity risks need to be considered holistically, with accountability that doesn't end at publication.
